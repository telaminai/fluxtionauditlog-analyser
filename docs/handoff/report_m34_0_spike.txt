REPORT — M34.0, the LangGraph spike against CURRENT code
Date: 2026-08-20 · Spec: docs/specs/spec-source-adapters.md · Tracker: M34.0
Code: tools/spikes/m34-langgraph/ (throwaway, out of the build — no analyser change was made)
Analyser: v1.7.0, unmodified. LangGraph 1.2.11 / langchain-core 1.6.0, read live before writing.

VERDICT: **YES — a foreign run is legible by today's tool, and M34.1–.4 are worth building.**
Every verb worked on a LangGraph run with ZERO analyser changes. The gate opens.

But the spike also did the second job it was commissioned for: it turned D-A1a from a review
inference into an observed fact, and it produced that evidence in the FIRST parallel super-step it
rendered, exactly as the tracker predicted. Read §3 before starting M34.1 — it changes what the SPI
has to carry.

================================================================================
1. WHAT WAS BUILT, AND WHY THIS SUBJECT
================================================================================
The supermarket POC's COLD-CHAIN subsystem, rebuilt on LangGraph: TemperatureReading and
CompressorTelemetry into fridge_monitor + compressor_health (parallel), joining at
temperature_forecast, conditionally reaching maintenance_scheduler. Same domain, same events, same
derived quantities as gregv12/supermarket-poc — a MATCHED PAIR rather than an arbitrary app,
because an arbitrary app only shows whether the analyser can open something. A matched pair shows
what the instrument LOSES on the way across, which is the finding.

The run: 24 chiller units × 30 cycles = 720 events, deterministic (no RNG — reproducible log).
Units CHILL-3, CHILL-11 and CHILL-19 drift hot and shake; the rest stay healthy.

The translator is ~120 lines and does two things: `stream_mode="debug"` → canonical record text,
and `graph.get_graph()` → GraphML. Nothing else.

================================================================================
3. THE FINDING — D-A1a CONFIRMED, AND IT IS WORSE ON SCREEN THAN ON PAPER
================================================================================
**All 720 records contain a concurrent super-step. Not some — all.** Every single record in this
log carries an order that no engine ever decided.

Stepping through record 74 (CHILL-3's first forecast breach) in the unmodified analyser:

    step 1: row=0  current='_concurrent'            row 1 / 5
    step 2: row=1  current='compressor_health'      row 2 / 5
    step 3: row=2  current='fridge_monitor'         row 3 / 5
    step 4: row=3  current='temperature_forecast'   row 4 / 5
    step 5: row=4  current='maintenance_scheduler'  row 5 / 5

The tool walks compressor_health BEFORE fridge_monitor and the topology paints dispatch-order
badges — 2 on one, 3 on the other — as though one caused the other. They ran concurrently in
super-step 1. That "order" is LangGraph's stream arrival order: scheduling detail, not causality.

On the Fluxtion side the identical presentation is MEANING — the AOT compiler derived that order and
step-through is reading it back. Here it is noise wearing meaning's clothes, and **nothing on screen
distinguishes the two cases.** A user who trusts the badge on a Fluxtion log is right; the same user
trusting the same badge on a LangGraph log is wrong, and has no way to tell.

This is UP-FLX-11's lesson one level up, and it is the single most important thing the spike found.
D-A1a (`ordering: TOTAL | PARTIAL` + a per-cycle concurrency marker) is NOT optional polish for
M34.1 — without it the adapter's first act is to fabricate causality.

**The workaround proves the point.** The translator emits a `_concurrent` marker line naming the
nodes that ran together, because the format has no field for it. In the app that marker is parsed as
an ordinary node: it appears in the step-through as row 1, it resolves as a data series
(`_concurrent.nodes` returns 720 points), and its node list is mangled into keys —
`ran: compressor_health, fridge_monitor: null` — because a comma-separated list in a value position
is key/value syntax. Concurrency smuggled through a field meant for something else does not survive
the trip. It needs a real representation.

================================================================================
4. THE SECOND FINDING — COVERAGE IS RIGHT AND ITS READING IS WRONG
================================================================================
coverage reported 4 of 6 covered, naming `__start__` and `__end__` as never-logged. Both figures are
correct and the conclusion a reader draws is false: those are LangGraph's synthetic pseudo-nodes,
not dead code. On the POC, "uncovered" found 54 genuinely dead nodes and that was the headline
finding. Here the same number means nothing.

So: **an adapter must not emit pseudo-nodes into the declared graph**, or must mark them as
structural. Coverage's value depends entirely on "declared" meaning "things that were supposed to
run", and every engine has its own scaffolding that isn't.

**Credit where it is due, and it matters:** the analyser CAUGHT the translator's synthetic node
without being asked to. coverage returned

    loggedButNotInTopology: ["_concurrent"]
    warning: "instanceIds in the log are absent from the topology — the graphml is probably from a
              different build, which makes every other figure here suspect"

It found a node the adapter invented, and rather than quietly excluding it, declared every other
figure in the same response suspect. The honesty disciplines transfer to a foreign source with no
modification at all. That is the strongest single argument in this report for M34 being worth
building: what generalises is not the file format, it is the refusal to state more than is known.

================================================================================
5. WHAT THIS CHANGES FOR M34.1–.4
================================================================================
5.1  D-A1a moves from "amendment" to PRECONDITION. The SPI cannot ship an ordering-blind
     `nodeLogs`; the consumers (step-through, route escalation, the M21 classification) must
     qualify loudly when ordering is PARTIAL. Ordering is not metadata about the run — on a
     concurrent engine it is the difference between evidence and fabrication.
5.2  The declared graph needs a STRUCTURAL/scaffolding flag so coverage can exclude an engine's
     own plumbing without an adapter having to lie about the node set.
5.3  D-A3 needs nothing. LangGraph's per-task `result` IS the attribution rule. Do not build
     machinery for a problem this source solves for free — but do check Temporal before
     generalising, because that is the engine the spec says has no declared graph at all.
5.4  The record↔cycle mapping is confirmed: one input invocation = one record, super-steps NESTED
     inside it. Mapping a super-step to a record instead would shatter one event into four and take
     goto, step-through and coverage down with it.

8. GLITCH-FREEDOM — LangGraph is NOT; Fluxtion is, and it is why the loop count differs
================================================================================
A *glitch* (the reactive-programming term) is a derived node observing inputs from different
generations — the diamond problem. The spike settles it for both engines by experiment, not by
reading marketing.

LANGGRAPH: NOT GLITCH-FREE. An unbalanced diamond (JOIN reads A one hop away and B three hops away):

    trace : ['A', 'B1', 'B2', 'JOIN(a=1.0, b=0.0)', 'B3', 'JOIN(a=1.0, b=9.0)']
    JOIN fired: 2 times

The first firing is a textbook glitch: a=1.0 is this generation, b=0.0 is the initial value. Pregel
schedules a node whenever ANY predecessor wrote in the previous super-step, so unequal path lengths
make a join run early on partial state, then again. The first result is wrong, and if the node has a
side effect (raising a maintenance job) it happens twice.

FLUXTION: GLITCH-FREE BY CONSTRUCTION. Three independent confirmations, in increasing strength:
  · docs/claude.txt states the compiler emits "a flat dispatch tree in topological order" — but
    stops short of a join-node contract, so this alone would be inference (rule 6).
  · The POC's log: 0 of 581 records has any node firing twice in one cycle.
  · DECISIVE — the generated source. handleEvent(TemperatureReading) touches 47 distinct nodes and
    invokes NONE of them more than once; every unbalanced join on that path (coldChainRisk,
    complianceLedger, costLedger, coldChainInTransit) appears exactly 1x, placed in topological
    order. The straight-line emission makes exactly-once structural: there is no scheduler to get
    it wrong at runtime.

================================================================================
9. THE LOOP COUNT — what "correct dispatch order" costs on each engine
================================================================================
Computed over the POC's real declared graph (311 nodes / 504 edges, longest-path layering):

    max depth (super-steps)              13
    join nodes (>1 predecessor)          59
    UNBALANCED joins                     37   <- each glitches, per §8
    extra firings per event              47
      on paths the full day exercises    34   <- findable by testing
      on paths that never ran             3   <- ship undetected
    hot examples: categoryPnl, coldChainInTransit, coldChainRisk, complianceLedger,
                  costLedger, dailyReport

37 sites, each silently computing a plausible number from incomplete input. The FIXING is cheap; the
DISCOVERY is the cost, because the symptom (a wrong aggregate) is far from the cause (a depth
mismatch several hops upstream) and there is no exception to grep for — §3's InvalidUpdateError only
fires on same-step channel collisions, not on stale reads across steps.

And the fixes INTERACT: padding a short branch to equalise depth changes every downstream depth and
can manufacture new unbalanced joins. It is iterate-to-fixpoint, not a checklist.

Estimate: 20-40 loops. On Fluxtion the number is ZERO — not small, absent, because the compiler
resolved it before the program ran.

================================================================================
10. RUNTIME — measured, same machine, same event type
================================================================================
                                            ns/event      events/sec
    Fluxtion  TemperatureRdg, no audit           424       2,359,837
    Fluxtion  TemperatureRdg, full audit       1,510         662,119
    LangGraph 4-node cold chain               483,000           2,072

~1,100x on the untraced comparison, and Fluxtion is traversing 309 nodes to LangGraph's 4 — roughly
44 ns/node against 121,000 ns/node, ~2,750x.

DO NOT read this as "LangGraph is slow." It is built for graphs whose nodes are LLM calls taking ~1
second, where 121 us of framework overhead is 0.01% and invisible. It becomes catastrophic only when
the node is arithmetic, which is the POC's shape. The two engines are sized for different node
costs, and that is the strategically useful reading: M34's adapters do not make agent graphs fast.
They give agent graphs the forensic instrument they currently lack.

(Fluxtion's own audit logging costs 3.6x on this path and 11.9x on SaleLine — the real price of the
log the analyser reads, still leaving 662k events/sec.)

11. TOKEN COST TO BUILD A CORRECT GRAPH OF COMPLEX DEPENDENCIES
================================================================================
Only engine-specific authoring counts. Domain logic (a node's arithmetic) is identical either way
and is NOT a Fluxtion cost — an earlier draft of this analysis wrongly included it.

    FLUXTION (measured)
      StoreGraphBuilder, code only            24,342 chars    ~7,400 tokens
      markup in domain classes                14,050 chars    ~4,300 tokens
        (82 @OnTrigger, 31 @OnEventHandler, 111 extends EventLogNode, 146 auditLog.)
      artifact total                          38,392 chars   ~11,600 tokens
      authoring spend (blog: 200->500 nodes)                 ~19,000 tokens  (1.6x artifact)
      dispatch-order correctness loops                                0
      ---------------------------------------------------------------------
      TOTAL                                                  ~20,000 tokens

    LANGGRAPH (projected from the spike's measured per-construct costs)
      State schema, 670 channels, ~1/3 needing reducers      ~7,000 tokens
      add_node x311 + add_edge x422                          ~8,200 tokens
      82 routers + add_conditional_edges                     ~5,800 tokens
      artifact total                          69,230 chars   ~21,000 tokens   (1.8x Fluxtion)
      correctness surcharge, 37 unbalanced joins:
        finding the PATTERN (first 1-3 sites)                ~50-100k tokens
        remaining ~34 sites at ~5k each                        ~170k tokens
        fixpoint re-checks after depth changes                  ~50k tokens
      ---------------------------------------------------------------------
      TOTAL                                                  ~250-300k tokens

    RATIO: roughly 15x, and the artifact is only 1.8x of it. The gap is not typing, it is the
    37 correctness loops that exist on one engine and not the other.

THE QUALIFIER THAT MATTERS: this assumes correctness to Fluxtion's standard — exactly-once,
glitch-free. Many agent workflows do not need it. If a node is an LLM call, firing it twice costs
money and is otherwise harmless, and a stale first read is often corrected by the second pass. For
that shape the surcharge is near zero and LangGraph is entirely reasonable. The 15x is the price of
a guarantee, and it is only worth paying where the guarantee is worth having.
